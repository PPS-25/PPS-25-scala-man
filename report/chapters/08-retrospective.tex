\chapter{Retrospective}

This chapter reviews the project as it was carried out: what each sprint delivered, how the process behaved in
practice, and what remains open. The team considers the outcome a success: \textbf{every epic of the product backlog
was delivered}, including the four that had been marked optional from the outset, none of which was dropped or reduced
in scope.

\section{Analysis of the development process}

Adopting an agile process was, together with the choice of Scala, the main novelty of the project for every member of
the team, and it proved a good fit for a project of this size.

\subsection{What worked}

The rhythm of the work rested on two weekly appointments: a mid-week backlog refinement and an end-of-sprint meeting,
in which the review, the retrospective and the planning of the following sprint took place one after the other. Every
planned appointment was held and, whenever a personal commitment required it, it was moved rather than skipped. Every
sprint closed with its own content delivered, with the tasks planned during planning completed within the agreed time.

In this setting daily meetings were not necessary. With weekly sprints and at least two meetings a week, the picture of
how the sprint was progressing was already up to date without a daily appointment: minor questions were settled in a
WhatsApp group dedicated to the project, the scrum board kept track of everyone's progress and could be consulted at
any time, and pull requests made the state of every area visible to all.

Backlog management relied entirely on the tools offered by GitHub: a scrum board for the state of progress, with epics
represented as tasks and split into subtasks, each carrying its own estimate, labels and priority. The team worked well
with this arrangement, which kept in one place what had been planned, what was in progress and what was left to do.

The roles agreed at the beginning were also carried through for the whole duration of the project. Alex Santini, as
Product Owner, maintained the scrum board and the repository configuration and was the one who most kept the meetings
on track; Matilde D'Antino, as client and domain expert, played the application at every increment and reported her
observations back to the rest of the group.

On the technical side, continuous integration was in place from the first sprint and was never switched off, so that a
failing test or a formatting deviation was reported before the change reached the shared branch; no change entered the
integration branch without a review by another member.

Assigning each epic to a single member finally gave everyone a coherent area to answer for and kept the design
decisions of an epic consistent even when they were taken over several weeks. A correction to the way pull requests
were used grew out of this arrangement: the initial plan called for one per individual task, but it became clear after
the first week that the number of reviews so generated weighed on the process more than it kept it under control. The
rule was therefore split into two cases. If an epic is followed by a single person, its tasks are developed on the
branch of the epic and one pull request brings it into \texttt{dev}; if instead the tasks are distributed over several
people, each has its own branch and its own pull request towards the branch of the epic, which is integrated only once
the epic is complete. The resulting reviews touch more files and demand more attention, but for a group of three people
on weekly sprints this cost less than the fragmentation it replaces.

\subsection{What proved harder}

The greatest difficulty was not technical but linguistic. Besides the novelty of the process itself, the team had to
take possession of a new vocabulary and learn to use it with awareness.

The second uncomfortable point was the difficulty of keeping tasks genuinely separate. Some tasks, and in a few cases
whole epics, needed others assigned to different members to be finished first, and the waiting that followed was at
times inconvenient. The clearest example concerns the enemy: three different epics needed the enemy as an entity, each
introduced it in the form that made its own work simple, and since the matter had never been discussed in depth the
concept had to be taken up again later to put it back in order.

\section{Future work}

Since the features initially intended as optional were implemented in full as well, the team discussed which others
could be introduced in the future. The possibilities are many; the ones considered are the following.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Graphics and gameplay experience.} A more polished visual appearance and a richer overall experience,
  together with mazes more elaborate than the ones shipped with the game.
  \item \textbf{The return of an enemy to its own spawn point.} An enemy defeated by the player under the effect of
  invulnerability leaves the game for good; making it reappear at the spawn point it was born from would turn the
  effect into a temporary advantage rather than a permanent one.
  \item \textbf{Frightened enemies after invulnerability.} At present, invulnerability protects the player from losing
  a life and allows an enemy to be defeated when the two meet. A natural extension would make the effect visible in the
  enemies' behaviour before a collision occurs: while it is active, each enemy would temporarily select moves that
  increase its distance from the player rather than following its normal pursuit, anticipation, or patrol strategy.
  This would require a short-lived \emph{frightened} movement strategy, chosen from the same walkable neighbours and
  teleport links used by the existing AI. The game would retain each enemy's normal strategy and, for a Patroller, its
  current corner target; when invulnerability expires, those choices would be restored unchanged. Thus the bonus would
  create a tactical opportunity to chase enemies without changing their long-term behaviour or disrupting a patrol
  route. If the player still reaches an enemy while the effect is active, the current defeat-and-score rule would
  continue to apply.
  \item \textbf{An integrated map editor.} Player-authored mazes are written as text files and validated on loading,
  which is sufficient but assumes the format documentation has been read first.
  \item \textbf{Further enemy strategies.} The abstraction accepts them without modification, as the patrolling enemy
  demonstrated; only the time available prevented more of them.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}

The final result is \textbf{a complete and playable game}: not a prototype, but an executable that the delivery
pipeline produces automatically and that anyone can download from the releases of the repository and run. The group is
satisfied with the work done, because every objective it had set itself was reached. Facing those objectives without
particular familiarity with either Scala or Scrum was a challenge, and one the team believes it carried through.

Collaboration between the members went very well, both because of everyone's willingness to discuss and because of the
contribution of the process, which supported it in a concrete way: the sprints imposed precise deadlines to meet and
the periodic meetings made the dialogue regular.

The way of working is the aspect of this experience the team appreciated most. Being the same process adopted in
industry, it is also the part that will most easily carry over into a real working context.
